01 / Coherent exchange · Eqs. (7.77)–(7.80)

A two-level atom exchanges one excitation with a single cavity mode. Kets are |field, atom⟩, with atomic ground state 0 and excited state 1. We use the propagator in Eq. (7.77), in a rotating frame and with ℏ = 1. The printed Hamiltonian (7.76) has inconsistent signs; the implementation follows (7.77) directly.

Ω = √(g² + δ²)
P(absorption | initial photon) = (g²/Ω²) sin²(Ωt)
χᵢ = arg[eⁱᵟᵗ(cos Ωt − i(δ/Ω) sin Ωt)]

At δ = 0, t = π/(2g) transfers a photon to the atom; t = π/(4g) creates maximal atom–field entanglement from a single excitation. The refractive phase is undefined when the photon survival amplitude vanishes. The units of g and δ are inverse time.

02 / A three-level mediator · Eqs. (7.81)–(7.86)

Two optical modes a and b couple a shared ground state 0 to excited states 1 and 2. The atom starts in 0. We retain all eight states in the invariant subspace of (7.82), including photon absorption by the atom.

Ωₐᵦ = √(δ² + gₐ² + gᵦ²)
φₐᵦ = arg[eⁱᵟᵗ(cos Ωₐᵦt − i(δ/Ωₐᵦ) sin Ωₐᵦt)]
χ₃ = wrap(φₐᵦ − φₐ − φᵦ)

For this mode we use U = exp(+iHt) with the blocks printed in (7.83)–(7.85), as stated below (7.85), to match (7.86) and the sign of Fig. 7.5. φₐ and φᵦ use the same expression with gᵦ = 0 and gₐ = 0, respectively. The phase curves are survival-amplitude phases, not a unitary gate on the reduced optical state. “Absorbed” means a coherent atomic excitation, which can return to the field.

03 / From phase to logic · Eq. (7.87)

In the separate ideal gate model, logical kets use the order |a,b⟩ = |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩. Each qubit can be encoded in polarization or dual rails; 0 does not mean the absence of the entire photon qubit. Mode a is the control; mode b is the target. The local phases are attached to their named modes.

U = diag(1, eiφᵦ, eiφₐ, ei(φₐ+φᵦ+Δ))
Ucorrected = diag(1, 1, 1, eⁱΔ)
CNOT = (I ⊗ H) Ucorrected(π) (I ⊗ H)

The textbook reports φₐ ≈ 17.5°, φᵦ ≈ 12.5°, and Δ ≈ 16° for its historical experiment. The “16°” preset uses these phases in a lossless illustration. “Ideal π” is an assumed interaction, not a prediction that the three-level model achieves a deterministic CNOT.

04 / Reading the results

Probability is |amplitude|². In gate mode, fidelity F = |⟨ψideal|ψ⟩|² compares the selected observation point with the ideal final output for the chosen input; it is a state fidelity, not an average gate fidelity. Concurrence C = 2|ad − bc| quantifies pure two-qubit entanglement.

In Kerr mode, the eight amplitudes describe the full atom–field state without postselection. Its bars group the three possible atomic levels. Phase values are principal angles in (−π,π]; a dash marks a vanishing survival amplitude. Curves break across phase branch cuts.

Reference: Nielsen & Chuang, Quantum Computation and Quantum Information, §7.5, pp. 297–309. Historical experiment: Turchette et al., Measurement of conditional phase shifts for quantum logic (1995).

Physical limitations: Nielsen & Chuang (the “Drawbacks” summary of §7.5) identify a trade-off: stronger atom–field coupling makes it harder to couple photons into and out of the cavity, limiting the ability to cascade gates. In §7.5.4, they also discuss absorption and reflection losses as obstacles to cascading gates in the experiment described there. This simulator illustrates idealized atom–field interactions and quantum gates; it is not a practical, general-purpose quantum computer.
